Case study

Handwashing Technique

Maritime · Health & Hygiene Training

An interactive course that teaches the WHO handwashing technique to people working on ships, then lets them practise it in a 3D simulation that gives feedback on how they did.

3 monthsDelivered, against the 4 months I estimated for the client
PM + DeveloperI managed delivery and built the course myself
Storyline 360 + UnityCourse in Storyline, 3D practice simulation in Unity
Why it matters→When to wash→How to wash→Practise in 3D

How we delivered a month early

2–3 slides written→Client review together→I build while the next batch is written→Repeat

We didn't wait for a finished storyboard. Once the first two or three slides were ready, we walked the client through them, took feedback on the spot, and I started building while the instructional designer carried on with the next set. Unity ran in parallel: our Unity developer began modelling the hands and other 3D assets while the storyboard was still being written, and a separate tracker listed every asset and which ones depended on others. The client reviewed the assets, then the environment outline I set up, then downloaded builds of the simulation as each scenario came together. Because feedback came right after each chunk instead of at the end, there was no big rework phase and no change in scope. It was our first Unity project, and the client's quick turnaround on reviews helped us finish a month ahead of the plan.

What I did

  • Project management: scoping, timeline estimate, coordinating the team, delivery
  • Graphic design and full course development in Storyline 360
  • Interactive step explorer, scenario slides and knowledge checks
  • Navigation that unlocks as learners progress

The team

  • Me, an instructional designer, QA and a Unity developer
  • 3D practice simulation built by our Unity developer, with the environment outline set up by me
  • Simulation gives a score and per-step feedback

Built for everyone

  • Keyboard navigation
  • Alt text and captions
  • Works on mobile

Case study

Vehicle Breakdown & Emergency Response

Fleet & Driver Safety · 3D Scenario Training

A 3D, game-style e-learning module for company drivers. Learners follow a driver through a working day, from the building entrance to the road, and then face a breakdown where the right steps matter more than speed. The client is confidential, so no names or branding appear here.

6 monthsFrom first scenarios to final delivery
PM + ID + QAI managed the project, wrote the learning and reviewed the module
Team of twoMe, plus a freelance Unity developer

The brief

Teach drivers what to do when a vehicle gets stuck, breaks down or an emergency happens on the road. It is procedural, safety-critical content: the order of the steps matters, and the first instinct (trying to fix the truck yourself) is the wrong one. Reading a list of rules would not be enough, so the module puts the driver inside the situation in a fully 3D environment with characters and vehicles.

A day in the life, then the breakdown

Enter the building, meet the security guard→Briefing room: vehicle and area assigned→Collect the truck→Drive out→Breakdown

Starting the module at the start of the shift gives the emergency a believable context. By the time the truck stops, the learner already knows the building, the people and the route.

What the driver has to do

Do not try to fix it→Pull the truck over→Place reflective warning lights behind and in front→Call the company→Wait well away from the truck until the rescue team arrives

How the work ran

I wrote the scenarios and the scenes myself and took the client through the storyboard slide by slide. The content was accepted with very few changes. The ones that came back were small details specific to how the company does things, and I worked those into the storyboard and the module. I also designed the character and vehicle samples, then coordinated with the freelance Unity developer who built the 3D work. As the project manager I reviewed the finished module and acted as QA. It was my second project as a project manager.

What I did

  • Project management and delivery
  • Scenario and scene writing
  • Storyboarding and client walkthroughs
  • Character and vehicle sample design
  • Facilitator guide
  • Module review and QA

The team

  • Me: project manager, instructional designer, QA
  • A freelance Unity developer, working remotely, who built the 3D environment

Result

  • Delivered in the planned six months
  • Client happy with the final module
  • Content approved with only minor, company-specific refinements

Case study

AI Video Production Workflow

Corporate training · Gemini Flow

Turning a still image into a video clip takes minutes. Keeping dozens of clips looking like the same film is the hard part. This is the process I use to keep characters, settings and style consistent in corporate training video, and to plan the credits it takes.

Gemini FlowMain platform for AI image and video generation
5-part promptScene summary, image, video, negative and continuity notes, every time
Locked referencesUploaded images are the source of truth, with no redesigns

The problem

AI video drifts. A clean-shaven character grows a beard halfway through a clip. The render style slides towards photorealism by the last second. A prop appears in someone's hand. In training content that matters: viewers notice, reviewers send the clip back, and every regeneration costs credits against a deadline. With large numbers of clips and tight turnarounds, failures and redos are part of the plan, not a surprise.

How a clip moves through the process

Locked references→Prompt from the template→Generate→Check for drift→Redo or approve→Hand off to edit

Keep it consistent

  • Every uploaded character and environment image is a locked reference. No redesign, no enhancement.
  • A style guide, a character bible with identity locks (face, hair, clothing, accessories) and an environment bible.
  • Portrait source images are extended to full widescreen, never stretched.
  • Negative prompts aimed at the drift I have actually seen, including style slipping at the end of a clip.

Keep it repeatable

  • One prompt format for every shot: scene summary, image prompt, video prompt with camera, movement, lighting and timing, negative prompt, continuity notes.
  • Animation kept subtle: blinking, breathing, small gestures. No exaggerated movement.
  • Lighting and camera language treated as one film, not separate clips.

Keep it on schedule

  • Credits worked out before starting, including a redo rate, with any shortfall raised early.
  • Client feedback sorted into categories and tracked until each item is closed.
  • An edit list for the editor, with callouts for known problem areas.
  • Audio checked against the approved script.

What I would tell a team starting out

  • Write each character down once and reuse the same words every time.
  • Name your known failure modes in the negative prompt instead of hoping they do not happen.
  • Plan for redos before the deadline, not during it.
  • Keep one approved script as the reference for every check.

Tools I built from this

Two small tools came out of the same problems. The Shot Prompt Builder inserts saved character and style text into every prompt. The Video Credit Planner works out credits, redos and daily targets for a batch.
